The 15-minute daily practice rule that top-scoring students swear by

3 min read · Updated Sep 2026

Most students overestimate what a single long study session can do, and underestimate what fifteen minutes a day, every day, adds up to. The second one is far easier to sustain — and that's exactly why it works.

Consistency beats intensity

A three-hour study session sounds more productive than fifteen minutes, but it's also far easier to skip when you're tired, busy, or just not in the mood — and once you skip one, skipping the next gets easier too. A short daily commitment is low-friction enough that it survives busy weeks, which means it actually happens far more often than the "big session" you keep postponing.

Over a month, fifteen focused minutes a day adds up to more total practice than most students manage with sporadic long sessions — and because it's spread out, it builds recall more effectively than the same time compressed into one sitting.

How to actually make it a habit

Attach it to something you already do

The easiest habits are the ones that piggyback on an existing routine — during breakfast, on the bus, right after you put your phone down for the night. Pick a moment that happens every day without you having to remember it, and do your practice there.

Make the bar embarrassingly low

Ten questions. Not a chapter, not an hour — ten questions. On a good day you'll often do more once you've started. On a bad day, ten questions still happened, and the streak survives. The goal of the 15-minute rule isn't depth in a single session, it's never having a day where you did nothing at all.

Track the streak, not the score

Early on, focus on whether you showed up today — not whether you got everything right. The score improves naturally as the habit compounds; what kills the habit is treating an off day as a reason to quit instead of just picking it back up tomorrow.

Why this matters more the closer boards get

As exams approach, it's tempting to switch to marathon sessions out of anxiety. But cramming under pressure is exactly the condition where the brain retains the least — and it stacks a huge amount of stress onto sessions that already feel unpleasant. Students who've already built a daily practice habit months earlier walk into the final weeks with a syllabus that already feels familiar, using that last stretch for genuine revision instead of learning material for the first time under pressure.
