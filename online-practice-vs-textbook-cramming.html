Why active practice beats passive reading (and how repetition builds retention)

6 min read · Updated Sep 2026 · By the MindVsYou team

Rereading a chapter for the third time feels like studying. It rarely is. If you have ever finished a reread feeling confident and then blanked on the same topic in a test, you have already met the gap between “this feels familiar” and “I can actually recall this.”

Quick answer

Active practice, answering questions from memory, beats passive reading because it forces recall, and recall is what strengthens memory and what the board exam tests. Repeating that practice across spaced days, and revisiting what you got wrong, improves retention far more than cramming the same material once.

Why is active practice better than passive reading?

When you reread notes, your brain recognises the material: you have seen these words, so they feel easy. That fluency gets mistaken for mastery. But recognising an answer in front of you is a different skill from producing it from memory when a question is asked cold, under time pressure. Board exams only test the second skill.

This is why cramming from a textbook the night before feels productive and evaporates fast: you practised recognition, not recall.

What is the testing effect?

The most reliable way to make information stick is to retrieve it. Each attempt to answer a question, right or wrong, followed by the correct answer, strengthens the memory pathway the exam will use. This is the testing effect: retrieval itself cements memory, more effectively than equal time spent rereading. It is also why a wrong answer followed by an explanation is often more useful than an easy right answer: realising a gap is when the correction sticks.

How does repeated practice improve retention?

Memory fades without use, and each successful recall slows the fading. Ten questions a day for two weeks builds recall far more reliably than one long session on the same material, because each attempt reinforces the last. This is often called spaced repetition: the gaps between revisits grow, and each revisit strengthens the memory. In practice:

  • Attempt a chapter's questions soon after studying it.
  • Revisit missed questions after two or three days, then after a week.
  • Return to old chapters every few weeks with a handful of questions.

A single re-read the night before mostly tests short-term memory, which fades once the exam is over.

How do you apply this to Class 10 Science?

  1. Study a chapter once, then close the book and test yourself with PYQs.
  2. Use questions in every format (MCQ, written, case-based) so the concept is retrieved in different ways.
  3. Space your revisits instead of massing them. See how to revise Class 10 Science.
  4. Keep it daily and short. The 15-minute rule makes it sustainable.

Why does online practice help?

A textbook does not ask you anything. It cannot tell you which answer was wrong, why, or what to revise next. Structured online practice fixes all three: every question is a retrieval attempt, every wrong answer comes with an explanation, and a system that tracks your mistakes can bring exactly those questions back later. It is also simply accessible: ten questions on your phone fit into a bus ride, a gap between classes or a spare break, so practice happens on days a full study session would not. Frequently asked questions

Is rereading useless for exams?

Not useless, but weak on its own. It is fine for a first pass or a quick refresh; pair it with questions to make sure you can recall, not just recognise.

What is active recall?

Active recall means pulling information out of your memory by answering a question or writing what you know, instead of just looking at it again.

How long should I wait before revisiting a wrong answer?

Two or three days is a good first gap, then about a week. The gap should be long enough that you have to work to remember, but short enough that you have not forgotten everything.

Does practising on a phone really work?

Yes, as long as you are answering questions and reviewing explanations, not scrolling. Short, frequent sessions are ideal for retention.
