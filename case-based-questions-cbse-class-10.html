How to practice case-based questions for CBSE Class 10 boards

5 min read · Updated Sep 2026

Case-based questions are the part of the CBSE paper that catches even well-prepared students off guard. You can know every formula and definition in the chapter and still lose marks here — not because the concept is hard, but because the format is unfamiliar. Here's how to get comfortable with it.

What makes case-based questions different

A case-based (or source-based) question gives you a short passage, a real-world scenario, an experiment description, a graph, or a data table — and then asks you two to four questions based on it. CBSE introduced this format as part of its shift toward competency-based assessment: instead of testing whether you memorised a fact, it tests whether you can apply a concept to a situation you haven't seen before.

That's exactly why they feel harder than they are. The concept being tested is usually something straight out of your textbook. The difficulty is entirely in reading comprehension and application — skills most students never specifically practice for a science or maths paper.

A method that actually works

1. Read the case before you look at the questions

It's tempting to jump straight to question (i) and scan the passage for the answer. Resist this. Read the full case first, and as you read, ask yourself: which chapter and which concept is this pointing to? Most cases are written around one or two core ideas — once you spot them, every sub-question becomes easier to place.

2. Underline the numbers and the nouns

In science and maths cases especially, the passage is often dense with specific details — quantities, conditions, named substances, stages of a process. These are rarely decorative. If a case mentions a specific temperature, pH value, or organism, there's a good chance one of the sub-questions depends on exactly that detail.

3. Answer each sub-question using only what's given — plus your syllabus knowledge

Case-based questions test two things at once: whether you understood the passage, and whether you can connect it to what you've studied. Don't answer purely from memory without referring back to the case, and don't try to answer purely from the case without applying the concept you were taught. The strongest answers do both — they cite something specific from the passage and explain it using the correct term or principle from the chapter.

4. Watch for questions that build on each other

Sub-questions within a single case are often sequenced — part (ii) sometimes depends on the reasoning you used in part (i). If your answer to an earlier part feels off, it's worth double-checking before you move on, since a mistake early in a case can quietly cost you marks on more than one sub-question.

Why practice volume matters more here than anywhere else

You cannot get comfortable with case-based questions by reading about the format — you get comfortable by seeing enough different cases that the pattern stops feeling unfamiliar. The passages change every time, but the underlying skill (read carefully, identify the concept, apply it) stays the same. That skill only builds with repetition.
